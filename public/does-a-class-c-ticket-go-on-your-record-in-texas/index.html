Does a Class C Traffic Ticket Go on Your Record in Texas?

Published November 1, 2026 · by Attorney Robert Navar

"Does this go on my record?" is the question we hear most often from El Paso drivers holding a Class C traffic ticket. The honest answer depends on what you mean by "record" and on how the case is resolved. A paid ticket becomes a conviction. A ticket dismissed after deferred disposition or a driving safety course does not. This post explains the difference between your Texas driving record and your criminal record, what shows up where, how long it stays, and the tools we use to keep a citation off both.

Two different records

Texas keeps two separate records that people lump together as "my record."

The first is your driving record, maintained by the Texas Department of Public Safety under your driver license number. It lists moving violation convictions, crashes, suspensions and license status. Insurers, employers who hire drivers, and courts pull this record.

The second is your criminal history record, the Computerized Criminal History (CCH) system that DPS maintains under Government Code Chapter 411. This is the database behind most official background checks. It is built from fingerprint-based arrest and disposition reports that courts and agencies must submit for Class B misdemeanors and above. Fine-only Class C offenses are generally not reported to the CCH because no fingerprints are taken on a traffic citation.

That leads many people to conclude a Class C ticket "is not on your criminal record." That is only partly true.

A Class C conviction is still a criminal conviction

A traffic ticket in Texas is a Class C misdemeanor under the Penal Code and the Transportation Code. It is prosecuted in a municipal court or justice court, it carries a fine of up to $500, and a guilty plea, a no contest plea, or a guilty verdict at trial produces a criminal conviction. Paying the ticket online or at the window is a plea of guilty or no contest; there is no "pay it and it goes away" option.

Although the conviction usually does not reach the DPS criminal history database, the court file itself is a public record. El Paso Municipal Court and the Justice of the Peace courts maintain searchable case records, and commercial background screeners routinely pull municipal and county court data. A Class C traffic conviction can therefore appear on a private background check. Some job applications also ask about any conviction, including Class C, and answering truthfully requires disclosing it.

What goes on the driving record and for how long

When you are convicted of a moving violation, the court reports the conviction to DPS under Transportation Code §543.203, and it is added to your driving record. The standard three-year driving record that insurers purchase shows moving violation convictions for three years from the date of conviction. The complete record retains the entry longer, and serious offenses such as DWI remain visible for much longer under separate rules.

Non-moving violations, such as expired registration or defective equipment, generally do not count as moving violations on the record, which is one reason we sometimes negotiate a moving violation down to a non-moving offense when a dismissal is not available.

Points and surcharges: repealed in 2019

For years, Texas added "points" to a driver's record for each moving violation conviction and billed an annual surcharge under the Driver Responsibility Program once a driver accumulated six points. That program was repealed by the Legislature effective September 1, 2019. There are no longer DRP points or annual surcharges for ordinary traffic convictions. Convictions still go on the driving record, and DPS can still suspend a license for an excessive number of convictions in a short period, but the point-and-surcharge system is gone. A website warning you about "points" on a Texas ticket is out of date.

What insurers see

Your insurance company does not call the court. It pulls your DPS driving record, typically at each renewal and whenever you apply for a new policy. Every moving violation conviction on that record is a rating factor. A single speeding conviction can raise premiums for three years; two or more can move a driver into a higher-risk tier. This is why a dismissed ticket is worth far more than the fine. A case resolved by deferred disposition or a driving safety course is dismissed and never reported as a conviction, so the insurer never sees it.

Deferred disposition: dismissal, not conviction

Code of Criminal Procedure article 45.051 lets a municipal or justice court judge defer a finding of guilt on a Class C offense for up to 180 days. You pay court costs and a special expense fee, comply with any conditions the judge sets, and avoid new citations during the period. If you complete it, the judge dismisses the case. There is no conviction, and nothing is reported to DPS as a conviction. If you do not complete it, the court can enter the conviction.

Deferred disposition is granted at the judge's discretion and is not available for every offense or every driver. It cannot be granted to a commercial driver license holder for a traffic offense, and some courts limit it for high-speed cases. The driving safety course: the other path to dismissal

Code of Criminal Procedure article 45.0511 gives most drivers the right to request dismissal by completing a six-hour driving safety course approved by the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation. You must request it by the appearance date, hold a valid Texas license and liability insurance, and not have used the option in the preceding twelve months. It is not available if the citation was for 25 miles per hour or more over the limit, for 95 miles per hour or more, or to a CDL holder, and certain offenses such as passing a school bus are excluded. On completion and proof of a current driving record, the court dismisses the case. Again, a dismissal is not a conviction. Expunction of a Class C after deferred disposition

Dismissal removes the conviction, but the court file showing the citation and the deferred disposition still exists. Texas law allows that file to be destroyed. The expunction statute, Code of Criminal Procedure Chapter 55A (formerly article 55.01(a)(2)(A)(ii)), specifically entitles a person to expunction of a Class C misdemeanor that was dismissed after completion of deferred disposition. There is no waiting period beyond the dismissal itself. The petition is filed in the appropriate court, and once granted, the court, DPS and any agency holding records of the citation must delete them, and you may lawfully deny the citation ever occurred in most settings. For a client whose career depends on a clean background check, we routinely recommend the expunction step after a deferred dismissal.

A conviction, by contrast, cannot be expunged. That is the single strongest reason not to pay a ticket outright.

Employers and commercial drivers

For most office jobs, a Class C traffic conviction is not disqualifying even when found. For anyone who drives for a living, it is different: employers pull the DPS driving record because their fleet insurer requires it, and a pattern of convictions can cost the job. Commercial driver license holders face the strictest rules: under 49 CFR 383.51 and §384.226, a court may not "mask" a CDL holder's conviction through deferred disposition or a driving safety course, every conviction goes on the CDL record, and two "serious traffic violations" in three years result in a federal disqualification. For CDL holders the only options are a dismissal on the merits, a negotiated reduction to a non-serious offense, or trial.

What this means for your ticket

A Class C ticket is not a parking ticket. Paid, it becomes a criminal conviction on your driving record for at least three years, visible to your insurer and discoverable by anyone who searches El Paso court records. Handled properly, it can be dismissed through deferred disposition or a driving safety course and, if needed, expunged so that no record remains. Frequently asked questions

Does a speeding ticket show up on a background check in Texas?

A paid or convicted speeding ticket is a Class C misdemeanor conviction. It is generally not reported to the DPS criminal history database, but the municipal or justice court record is public, and commercial background screeners that search El Paso court records can find it. A ticket dismissed through deferred disposition or a driving safety course is not a conviction.

How long does a traffic ticket stay on your driving record in Texas?

A moving violation conviction appears on the standard DPS driving record for three years from the date of conviction, which is the record most insurers pull. The complete DPS record retains it longer. Dismissed citations are not reported as convictions.

Can I expunge a traffic ticket in Texas?

If the ticket was dismissed after you completed deferred disposition, the Code of Criminal Procedure entitles you to an expunction of the Class C offense, which destroys the court and agency records of the citation. A ticket that was paid or resulted in a conviction cannot be expunged.

Are there still points on a Texas driver license?

No. The Driver Responsibility Program, which assigned points and annual surcharges, was repealed effective September 1, 2019. Convictions still appear on the driving record and can still lead to a suspension for an excessive number of convictions, but there is no longer a point-and-surcharge system.
